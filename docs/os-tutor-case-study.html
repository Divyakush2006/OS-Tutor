OS Tutor: Fine-Tuning a Small Language Model into a Verified Operating Systems Tutor

A 4-billion-parameter model that explains operating-system concepts, teaches Socratically, and delegates every calculation to deterministic simulators.

AuthorRegister number
Divyakush Punjabi24BCB0126

Summary

General-purpose language models state the rules of operating systems correctly but fail to execute them over many steps, which makes them unreliable tutors for a subject assessed through worked numerical problems. This project fine-tunes Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on an 11,760-row dataset built by an eleven-stage, gate-checked pipeline, and pairs the model with verified simulators that compute every procedural answer. The system is fully built and deployed locally. On 57 hand-written problems it scores 63.2% when the model answers alone and 100% (57/57) with verified delegation. General knowledge is retained within 0.75 percentage points of the base model. One open weakness — declarative OS theory, 4.29 points below the base model — is the focus of the next phase.

1.Introduction

1.1 Background

Operating systems is taught and examined largely through procedures. Students are asked to build a Gantt chart for Round Robin scheduling, count page faults under LRU, trace a disk head through SCAN, or decide whether a Banker's algorithm state is safe. Each is a fixed rule applied over many dependent steps, and a single wrong step invalidates every step after it.

Large language models are an attractive tutoring medium because they explain fluently, answer at any hour, and can adapt their explanation to a student's attempt. The question this project asks is whether a small model, trainable on free hardware and runnable on a student laptop, can be made trustworthy enough to tutor this subject.

1.2 The problem

A general model fails an OS student in three distinct ways:

  • It executes procedures unreliably. It can state the rule for SRTF correctly and still produce the wrong schedule. In our own measurements, every one of the 7 scheduling failures on a hand-written suite contained correct arithmetic paired with a wrong schedule.
  • It gives answers away. A student who shows a wrong attempt needs to be guided to the error, not handed the corrected answer.
  • Fine-tuning can damage it. Training a model narrowly on OS material risks eroding the general ability that makes its explanations good — catastrophic forgetting.

The first failure is architectural rather than a gap in knowledge, as Section 2.3 shows. It cannot be solved by adding more training data: scheduling had the most training rows of any category (1,733) and the worst accuracy (42%), while disk scheduling had 990 rows and 100%.

2.Literature Review

The design draws on four bodies of work: efficient fine-tuning, catastrophic forgetting, the limits of procedural reasoning in language models, and language models used for tutoring. Each subsection closes with how the finding shaped this project.

2.1 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

LoRA [1] freezes the pretrained weights and learns a low-rank update, reducing trainable parameters by orders of magnitude. QLoRA [2] stores the frozen base in 4-bit NormalFloat and back-propagates through it into LoRA adapters, which makes fine-tuning feasible on a single consumer or datacentre GPU. NEFTune [3] adds uniform noise to embeddings during training and measurably improves instruction-following quality at no inference cost.

LIMA [4] showed that a small set of carefully curated examples can align a strong base model, supporting the view that data quality matters more than volume.

Applied here: QLoRA made training possible on free Kaggle T4 GPUs; NEFTune (α = 5) is enabled; and the LIMA principle informed a heavily curated pipeline — though applied to knowledge categories only, since procedural skill still benefits from many distinct worked examples.

2.2 Catastrophic forgetting

Neural networks trained sequentially on new data lose earlier competence, a phenomenon first characterised as catastrophic interference [5]. Regularisation approaches such as Elastic Weight Consolidation [6] penalise movement of weights important to earlier tasks, but require estimating parameter importance over the original training distribution, which is unavailable for a pretrained model.

Four recent results are more directly usable:

  • LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning, because the low-rank constraint bounds how far the weights can move [7].
  • Self-distillation (SDFT) [8] rewrites training targets in the base model's own words, shrinking the distribution gap that drives forgetting.
  • SuRe [9] prioritises replay of the most surprising sequences — those with the highest negative log-likelihood — which are the ones most at risk of being lost.
  • FOREVER [10] observes that forgetting in language models follows an Ebbinghaus-like curve, and spaces replay frequently early in training and increasingly sparsely later.

Applied here: the anti-forgetting design combines all four — LoRA as the structural constraint, SDFT targets for theory and tutoring, 16% replay data ranked by surprise, and replay spaced on a forgetting-curve schedule — and it is measured with paired item-level flips rather than assumed.

2.3 Limits of procedural reasoning in LLMs

Samiei et al. [11] measured long-horizon execution of explicit finite-state machines and found that even Qwen3-235B reaches only about 50% task accuracy, degrading as the number of steps grows. The failure is in executing a known rule, not in knowing it.

Guo, Wu and Yiu [12] explain why. They bound the state-tracking capacity of decoder-only attention by O(H · log(L/H) · √dh), where H is the number of attention heads and dh the head dimension, and identify a deterministic horizon d* between 19 and 31 steps beyond which extended chain-of-thought degrades. Across 12 models, tool-integrated reasoning reached 86–94% accuracy against 24–42% for purely neural chain-of-thought.

Kambhampati et al. [13] argue on independent grounds that language models cannot reliably verify their own plans, and propose LLM-Modulo systems in which the model generates and an external, sound verifier checks.

Applied here: this is the central design decision of the project. Because the limit is architectural, no amount of fine-tuning removes it — so the model is never asked to compute. It reads the question, deterministic simulators compute the answer, and the model explains the result.

2.4 Language models as tutors

MathDial [14] collected tutoring dialogues in which teachers guide students who hold genuine misconceptions, and showed that off-the-shelf models tend to reveal answers too early rather than scaffolding the student's reasoning. Its annotated teacher moves — probing, focusing, telling — give a vocabulary for what good tutoring looks like.

Applied here: MathDial supplies real teacher moves for the tutoring category, and the OS half of that category is built the same way: each dialogue starts from a wrong attempt generated from a named misconception, and any reply that reveals the final answer is rejected.

No prior work we found combines a fine-tuned small model, verified procedural delegation, enforced Socratic behaviour and measured forgetting resistance in a single deployable tutor for operating systems.

3.Objective

Primary objective: build an offline operating-systems tutor that is numerically exact, pedagogically honest, resistant to catastrophic forgetting, and trainable entirely on free hardware.

The primary objective breaks down into six measurable objectives. Each has a fixed target, and the current status is reported against it:

O2Socratic integrity: a shown attempt is taught, not answeredCorrect routing of every attemptAchieved — 28/28, zero misroutes
O3No catastrophic forgetting of general abilityGeneral MMLU within 1 pp of baseAchieved — −0.75 pp

What the tutor is not. It is deliberately not a retrieval-augmented chatbot over a textbook. Retrieval returns text that resembles an answer; it cannot certify that a Gantt chart for a student's specific numbers is correct, because that chart exists in no document. The tutor computes the answer instead.

4.1 Collection strategy

4.3 Generated data

Public data for OS numericals is scarce and rarely verified, so the procedural category is generated by simulators written for this project. Every answer is computed, never written by hand or by a model. The simulators cover 39 question types across 8 families:

  • CPU scheduling — FCFS, SJF, SRTF, Round Robin, priority, preemptive priority, HRRN
  • Page replacement — FIFO, LRU, OPT, CLOCK
  • Deadlock — Banker's safety and request, including unsafe states
  • Disk scheduling — FCFS, SSTF, SCAN, LOOK, C-SCAN, C-LOOK
  • Contiguous allocation — first, best and worst fit
  • Address translation, and 11 formula kinds — effective access time, multilevel paging, deadlock minimum, fork count and others
  • Clarification — problems deliberately missing a required input, where the correct behaviour is to ask rather than guess

Two further categories are partly generated. Conceptual answers are self-distilled [8] from the base model, then sanitised and fact-checked; any answer asserting a known misconception is dropped, never repaired. OS tutoring dialogues begin from a wrong attempt produced by a named misconception — for example, applying non-preemptive SJF to an SRTF question.

5.Dataset Processing

5.1 Pipeline overview

Raw data passes through eleven stages, run by a single command. Each stage writes its statistics to a manifest, so every number in this section is taken from the build record rather than estimated.

5.2 Analysis before cleaning

Before any row is removed, each category is analysed on its own. Every candidate cleaning rule is tested against the data and adopted only if the evidence supports it — so the rules follow the data rather than an assumption about it. The analysis found, for example:

CategoryDefects foundRules rejected on evidence
Procedural434 missing a final-answer marker; 278 with no visible steps—
Tutoring283 non-alternating roles; 262 dead tails; 212 never ask the student a question; 31 leak the answer in the first turn“answer shorter than question”
Shell181 vague instructions; 126 bash syntax errors; 70 editor keystrokes“ends mid-sentence”, “brace placeholder”

Rejecting a rule is as important as adopting one. A tutoring rule that dropped replies shorter than the question looked sensible, but the analysis showed it would remove good Socratic prompts, which are short by design.

5.3 Cleaning, deduplication and decontamination

Cleaning kept 70,170 of 80,732 rows. The largest reasons for removal were:

A further 261 rows were repaired rather than dropped, and 707 pieces of personal information were redacted — 377 card numbers (Luhn-checked), 180 e-mail addresses, 149 phone numbers and one social-security number.

Deduplication removed 656 exact duplicates (blake2b hashing) and 1,133 near-duplicates (MinHash LSH at Jaccard 0.80).

Decontamination compared training data against all 4,836 evaluation items and removed 643 rows. Prose rows are matched on distinctive 13-grams, ignoring 93 boilerplate n-grams common to many rows. Generated problems are matched on an exact parameter fingerprint, which also catches a reworded leak of the same problem — 15 such matches were found. Without this step, evaluation scores would be inflated by memorised answers.

5.6 Rectifier ordering and spaced replay

Training data is read in a fixed order rather than shuffled, and that order is itself engineered. The rectifier interleaves categories so that no skill is learned in one uninterrupted block and then decays while another is learned:

Ordering constraintLimitMeasured
Longest run of one category44
Longest run from one source dataset (81 sources)31
Replay spacing, early → late in training—every 4 → every 14 rows
Spacing violations00

Replay is spaced densely early and sparsely later, following the forgetting-curve schedule of FOREVER [10], and within the replay pool examples are ranked by surprise, following SuRe [9].

6.Model Architecture Design

6.1 Design principle

The model explains; simulators compute. Following Section 2.3, the language model is never asked to carry out a procedure it cannot execute reliably. A fine-tuned 4B model handles reading, language and pedagogy; deterministic simulators handle every number. They are joined by an extraction step that turns a question into typed parameters.

The router reads the question first. A shown attempt outranks every other signal and routes to tutoring — and the tutoring path has no access to the solver or the fact checker, so there is no route by which a computed answer can reach a student who is still working.

6.2 Base model selection

The base model is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 [15], Apache-2.0 licensed. It was selected for four reasons: its non-thinking variant writes reasoning as visible steps a student can read; its 262,144-token native context comfortably covers 16,384-token training sequences; at 4B it fits QLoRA training on a 16 GB T4; and it scores strongly for its size — MMLU-Pro 69.6, MMLU-Redux 84.2 and GPQA 62.0 on its model card, where it is benchmarked against the 30B-parameter Qwen3-30B-A3B.

A study of the 10–20B range confirmed the choice for the current hardware. Figures below are estimates or vendor claims, not measurements on our machines:

AlternativeReason not selected
Dense 14B (Qwen3-14B, Phi-4)About 16–17 GB for QLoRA at 16,384 tokens — over a single T4 — and about 5 tokens/s served from 8 GB VRAM

Training behaved as intended. Validation loss fell monotonically from 0.4413 to 0.2713 over the epoch, and the final training loss (0.2710) sits almost exactly on the validation loss (0.2754) — the signature of a model that fitted the task without memorising its training traces.

6.4 Verified-solver layer

The solver turns a 63% model into a 100% system in three stages:

  1. Extract. The question is parsed into typed JSON parameters. Extraction deliberately runs on the untuned base model, which read parameters correctly 84% of the time against 79% for the fine-tuned adapter — the adapter is biased toward solving rather than reading.
  2. Ground. Every extracted number must appear in the question. An invented parameter causes the question to fall back to the model; a missing input produces a clarifying question instead of a guess.
  3. Solve and render. A deterministic simulator computes the result, and the answer is written in the same worked-steps format the model was trained on, so the student sees no seam.

A separate fact checker holds ten canonical corrections for misconceptions observed in real model answers — for instance, that LRU never suffers from Belady's anomaly — and rewrites a draft that asserts one.

7.Conclusion

The project has reached a working, deployed system. Its central claim — that a small fine-tuned model can tutor operating systems exactly if it delegates computation — is now supported by measurement. One weakness is open, and it is reported here in full.

7.2 Findings

  1. The accuracy ceiling was execution, not knowledge. Delegating computation lifted the suite from 63.2% to 100% with no retraining, and cut a full evaluation from 76 minutes to about 150 seconds.
  2. The anti-forgetting design worked on general ability. General knowledge moved 0.75 points, within scoring noise, and adjacent computer science improved, with 19 items gained against 11 lost.
  3. Socratic behaviour is enforceable by architecture. Routing, not prompting, guarantees that a student's attempt is never met with a computed answer.
  4. Procedural skill rose while declarative OS recall dipped. This was not anticipated: the loss that was feared in general knowledge appeared instead, slightly, in OS theory.

7.3 Limitations

  • The OS theory result rests on 70 questions, where four items decide the difference, and the base model was already near ceiling at 90%. It needs a larger set before it can be called settled.
  • Comparisons of the base model and the previous adapter on the procedural suite are outstanding. An earlier measurement was invalidated by a harness fault and withdrawn rather than reported.
  • The conceptual half of tutoring has no external verifier and no rubric-based measurement yet.
  • The fact checker holds a finite list of corrections and cannot catch an error nobody has written a rule for.
